Community as Curriculum

I led a six week Challenge Based Learning experience, mid year at the Apple Developer Academy Bali, where 220 learners went into 25 communities across Bali, listened, and built apps for the Apple ecosystem with the people who inspired them. I ran it alongside a team of planners, mentors, and ops who helped bring it to life.

Overview

The community was the inspiration, not the brief

I didn't want learners to arrive with a solution, or even a problem they'd already decided to fix. I wanted them to arrive to simply listen, understand, and see what could inspire them and challenge them to make something better.

The hardest part of leading this was holding the relationship as something real. The key differentiation my team and I worked hardest to draw, and to communicate clearly to both our learners and the communities themselves, was the difference between a community and a client. A client hands you requirements; a partner hands you their trust.

The community was not a client to serve. It was a partner to learn from, and a person to be inspired by.

Where it sits and why it matters

A young academy earning its place

I placed this challenge in the middle of the Academy year. By then learners could build, and they had the confidence to be trusted in someone else's world. It was the moment they were ready to leave the building, with enough skill to make something real and enough footing to represent us well.

The Academy is young, only around two years in Bali, and part of what we are still doing is situating ourselves here: learning the island, earning a name, and building relationships that outlast any cohort. Every learner who walked into an organisation was representing the Academy, often for the first time that organisation had heard of us. Designing the challenge, I had to think about how a two year old academy shows up in a culture with far deeper roots than its own.

And there is a deeper layer, really about belonging. Many of our learners travel to Bali from across Indonesia and around the world, and arrive without much connection to the island. A lot of our mentors and staff are new here too. So this was never only about learners meeting communities. It was about a whole group of newcomers, learners, mentors, and staff alike, forging genuine relationships with the place they now live, and beginning to belong here rather than simply pass through. I designed the learning outcomes and the sense of belonging as the same thing, not two.

The community was not a client to serve. It was a partner to learn from, and an endless source of inspiration

It starts with a choice

Engagement is the core of the whole challenge

Before getting to the core of the challenge, I wanted the decision of which community to join to belong to the learners. I had each mentor make a short video introducing their community, and it was that showcase, one video per community, that teams watched before choosing the one that pulled at them. Real engagement comes from finding a community you already care about. A learner who chose the sea turtle project because they love the ocean shows up completely differently from one who was handed it.

Engage

A big idea and a challenge to empower the community. The learner's choice drives engagement.

Investigate

Go into the community and gather real evidence, not assumptions.

Act

Build, test, iterate and share an evidence based response with an authentic audience.

Everything hard that comes later, the nervous and excited first visit, the deadlines, the pivots, become survivable because the learner picked this. Designing for engagement at the very start is how I made the other six weeks possible.

Designing the challenge

Partner, not client

Holding the relationship

I assigned one mentor to each community, someone who could form that deeper relationship over time. That pathway became the constant source of communication, and it was how I brought learners into the fold with their community.

A pipeline of willing communities

I mapped around 50 Bali organisations, built a careful two stage way of reaching out, and put enough logistics behind it to keep the partnership and intentions clear, protecting against any complex conversations that could arise later.

I profiled around 50 Bali organisations for fit and the need they might surface.

The two stage ask

Logistics

Continued Engagement

Designing the challenge

Three Visits

I structured every community relationship around three visits, each with a different job to do.

Visit 1

Explore

No agenda, just be there and experience the community on its own terms.

Visit 2

Go Deep

Go deeper through interviews and real conversation, now that some trust exists.

Visit 3

Test & Understand

Bring something back, test it with real users, and understand whether it actually helped.

The milestones, and video as synthesis

Each milestone forced a point of view

Key milestone

Challenge Response

A challenge the team set for itself, drawn from what they had seen and who had inspired them, not a brief handed down.

Key milestone

Research Synthesis

Interviews and fieldwork distilled into a clear point of view on the real need, replacing the assumptions the team walked in with.

Key milestone

Coded Prototype

A working app in the Apple ecosystem, not a mockup, built well enough to put in front of the community and react to.

Key milestone

Testing Evaluation

A verdict from real use with real people: whether it actually helped, and what that changed about the team's understanding of the problem.

In the age of AI, when anyone can generate the output, the crucial skill is articulating and synthesising your own learning.

A challenge built as a series of synthesis milestones is one of the most useful things I've designed. It keeps forcing the learner to metabolise the experience rather than just produce an artifact.

I set the synthesis as a two minute video on Photo Booth. The constraint is the design: on a webcam, in two minutes, you cannot hide a thin idea or fake having listened. And making it together bonded the teams. Deciding their story shoulder to shoulder is what turned a group assigned to each other into a team.

The ending

We held an exhibition, but the real moment of learning came after it.

Learners showed what they had built to an authentic audience, and that stage mattered. Then I invited every community back to the Academy, and learners sat down with their partners to debrief: what they had learned, what surprised them, what the community had given them. That conversation, not the demo, was the point. The apps were on the table, but they were not the centre of it.

If the exhibition had been the finale, the lesson would have been that the product is what matters. By making the closing moment a shared reflection, I wanted to teach that the relationship is what matters, and that the community is a source of inspiration to be thanked, not a box that got ticked.

Reflections

What shines looking back

What shines for me, looking back, is that we were able to build real relationships across all 25 communities. Hearing the small stories and the moments shared between our learners and the people they worked with was a genuinely amazing experience, and knowing those communities wanted to come back to the Academy, excited by what our learners had built, was fantastic.

What's just as remarkable is watching many of those relationships carry on well beyond the challenge. Learners and mentors are still in touch with communities, joining their events and staying involved long after the six weeks were up. It's proof that 220 learners now know the best work starts by being genuinely moved by someone else.
